# Selling in Spanish: Spanish-preferring business owners in the US

For any project that sells to Spanish-preferring owners. Sources in SOURCES.md, "Spanish selling". Project facts (price, terms, live offer, what the product can do in Spanish) come from projects/<project>/CONTEXT.md and win over anything here.

**Read this first.** Culture notes below are tendencies, not rules about any one person. Latino owners in the US come from many countries (Cuba, Venezuela, Colombia, Mexico, Puerto Rico, the Dominican Republic, Central America and more), born here or newly arrived. Treat each owner as a person. When unsure, ask.

---

## 1. When to switch to Spanish

Switch when the owner does one of these: writes to you in Spanish, gives you a Spanish line, answers the phone in Spanish, or asks. Don't switch because of a last name or an accent. Many bilingual owners prefer English for business, and guessing can feel like profiling.

If unsure, ask once, both ways: "¿Prefiere que sigamos en español o en inglés?" / "Happy to do this in English or Spanish, whichever you like."

**Be honest about your own Spanish.** If the person on the call can't hold a whole sales call in Spanish, say so in simple Spanish and offer what you can do well: the call in English with a short written Spanish summary, or a later call with a Spanish speaker. A rough call that pretends fluency costs more trust than a clear "mi español es básico". Martha drafts written Spanish; a fluent person should read anything long before it goes out.

**Be honest about what you can and can't do in Spanish (per project).** List it in the honest-limits section of projects/<project>/CONTEXT.md: which parts of the product, service, support and paperwork work in Spanish today, and which don't. If the owner or their customers need Spanish and a part isn't available, say so before they decide. Never promise a Spanish version, or a date for one, until the owner of the project confirms it.

---

## 2. Usted vs tú

- **Default to usted** with any owner you haven't met, in speech and in writing. It signals respect (respeto), not coldness.
- **Follow their lead.** If the owner switches to tú, you can follow after a turn or two. Staying in usted is never wrong; jumping to tú first can be.
- **Regional detail.** Sociolinguistic research on Cuban Spanish in Miami (Lynch, University of Miami) found the most recent arrivals favor usted most, and tú spreads the longer people live in the US. So a recent arrival likely expects usted; a second-generation owner may switch to tú quickly. Let them choose.
- **Keep it consistent.** Don't mix "usted" and "tu página" in one message. Check every verb and possessive: su, le, lo, usted.
- **Names and titles.** Use the first name they give, with usted ("Marta, ¿usted...?"). Use "don" or "doña" only if others use it with them or they're clearly older and it fits. "Señor" or "señora" plus a last name is fine on first contact by phone.

---

## 3. Trust and relationship first

Three values come up again and again in work with Latino communities. Use them to guide behavior, never as tactics.

- **Personalismo.** Business happens between people, not companies. Spend the first minute as a person: a real greeting, a real question about their week, before any business. Skipping it reads as cold.
- **Confianza.** Trust earned by keeping small promises over time. Call exactly when you said. Send exactly what you said. Don't change terms between calls. One missed callback can end it.
- **Respeto.** Polite forms, no pushing, no talking down, no interrupting. Pressure tactics that work on some buyers backfire hard here.

What this changes in the process:
- The up-front contract still works, said warmly: "No le quiero quitar mucho tiempo."
- Discovery can run slower, with more listening. Fine. Make the offer shorter instead.
- Referrals inside the community are worth more than any ad. A happy owner tells other owners. So does an unhappy one.
- Ask for the decision gently, and accept "lo tengo que pensar" with a dated check-back, never a squeeze.

---

## 4. Family business norms

- Many small businesses are family-run. A spouse often keeps the books or answers the phone, sons or brothers run crews, and a parent may still weigh in.
- "Lo tengo que hablar con mi esposa" is usually a real shared decision, not a brush-off. Respect it, offer to explain to both, and never go around the family member.
- The person who answers the phone may be the spouse. Treat them as a decision-maker, because they often are.
- Don't make a family member look bad ("your wife misses calls"). The problem is the volume and the hours, never the person.
- Keep promises to the whole family the same way: one set of terms, in writing, the same in Spanish and English.

## 5. WhatsApp as a channel

- **Why it matters.** Pew Research Center's 2025 social media survey found WhatsApp use is highest among Hispanic adults in the US (56%). Many owners run their business chat on it.
- **Only with permission.** Message on WhatsApp only when the owner gave you that number for contact, or wrote to you there first. No cold WhatsApp, no bought lists, no adding people to groups or broadcast lists. WhatsApp's business messaging policy requires opt-in for business-started messages; check the current policy before scaling. Treat it like text messaging under PLAYBOOK red line 4: 8am to 9pm their time, and honor any "no más" or "pare" at once.
- **Style.** Short. One idea, one question. Usted. Greet first. No links in the first message. Voice notes are normal there; if the owner sends one, a short reply or a call back is fine, but never a long robotic voice note.
- **Who sends.** Martha drafts. A human sends from the business's phone. Never claim to be human if they sincerely ask.

## 6. Core lines in Spanish (usted, neutral Latin American)

Templates. Fill the braces before sending. Every price, term and offer must match projects/<project>/CONTEXT.md on the day you use it, and must say exactly what the English version says.

**Opener, phone (they replied to you):**
> "Buenas tardes, ¿hablo con {nombre}? Le habla {su nombre}, de {empresa}. Usted nos respondió sobre {el tema}. ¿Tiene unos minutos ahora, o prefiere que lo llame más tarde?"

**Opener, WhatsApp (only on a number they gave you):**
> "Hola {nombre}, le escribe {su nombre}, de {empresa}. Gracias por responder. ¿Prefiere que hablemos por aquí o por teléfono?"

**Up-front contract:**
> "No le quiero quitar mucho tiempo. Le hago unas preguntas sobre {el tema}, y le digo con sinceridad si le podemos ayudar. Si no le sirve, me lo dice sin problema. ¿Le parece bien?"

**Discovery (one at a time, adapt to the project):**
> "Cuando usted está ocupado y llega un cliente nuevo, ¿qué pasa?"
> "Más o menos, ¿cuántos clientes se le escapan en una semana de mucho trabajo?"
> "¿Cuánto vale un trabajo típico para usted, más o menos?"
> "Después que manda un presupuesto y el cliente no contesta, ¿qué hace?"

**The gap (their numbers only):**
> "A ver si le entendí bien. Cuando usted está ocupado, {lo que pasa}, y el cliente se va con otra compañía. Si se le va un solo trabajo al mes así, son unos {su número} que se pierden. ¿Es así?"

**The offer (fictional Brightpane example; use your CONTEXT.md):**
> "Nosotros nos encargamos de que sus ventanas estén limpias cada mes, sin que usted tenga que pensar en eso. La primera limpieza cuesta $149, después desde $99 por visita, y puede cancelar cuando quiera."

Add only if live and they qualify: "Como {condición real}, tiene {descuento real} hasta el {fecha real}."

**Honest limit (say it before they ask, if it applies):**
> "Le soy sincero: {lo que todavía no funciona en español}. Antes de que usted decida, le averiguo si eso va a cambiar y cuándo."

**Next step:**
> "Para empezar necesito {lo que hace falta}. ¿Le queda bien el {día} a las {hora}?"

**Not now:**
> "No hay problema. ¿Cuándo le parece bien que lo vuelva a llamar?"

**Clean exit:**
> "Entendido, no le vuelvo a escribir. Que le vaya bien."

**Usage notes.** "Presupuesto" is the standard word for a quote and is understood everywhere; some owners say "estimado", which is fine if they use it first. "Obra" or "trabajo" for a job. "Buzón de voz" for voicemail. "Reseñas" for reviews. "Lo vuelva a llamar" is the common Latin American form ("le vuelva a llamar" can sound like Spain). "Le queda bien" for "does it work for you". Avoid "aplicar", "realizar" and other stiff translation words.

## 7. What not to do

1. **No machine translation sent raw.** Translate the idea, then have a fluent person check anything over two sentences.
2. **No tú first.** No "amigo", "papi", "mi hermano", "jefe" with someone you just met.
3. **No regional slang that isn't theirs.** Avoid Mexico-only words ("ahorita", "chido", "órale") unless they use them, Spain forms ("vosotros", "vale"), and words that are rude in some countries. Plain, neutral Spanish is safest.
4. **No Spanglish in writing** unless they write that way first.
5. **No assumptions about origin, status or politics.** Never ask where they're "really" from, about immigration status, or about home-country politics. Don't guess nationality from an accent.
6. **No faking fluency or culture.** Don't claim shared heritage or community ties you don't have (Cialdini's unity used dishonestly).
7. **No overpromising in Spanish.** No "everything works in Spanish" unless CONTEXT.md says it does.
8. **No pressure.** No fake urgency, no squeezing past "lo tengo que pensar" or "lo hablo con mi esposa".
9. **No cold WhatsApp, no calling a Spanish line uninvited.** Only the channels and numbers they gave you.
10. **No skipping the greeting.** A message that starts with the price reads as rude.
11. **No different terms by language.** The Spanish offer says exactly what the English one says.
