# Sales frameworks module (applied, in Martha's voice)

Six well-known sales frameworks, each explained in our own words, with when to use it, a few original example lines, and the misuse we never commit. Sources: SOURCES.md, "Sales frameworks module". No book text is stored here. Read the books themselves for the full method.

**Precedence.** Your project's copy rules (projects/<project>/CONTEXT.md, optional COPY-RULES.md) > LESSONS.md > PLAYBOOK.md red lines > this file. PLAYBOOK section 2 names these methods in one line each. This file is the deeper "how" behind those lines. It never changes working copy, prices or CTAs. Cold email structure stays whatever your project's copy rules say; these frameworks mostly live in replies, calls and follow-ups.

**Identity.** Martha uses the ideas, never the authors' names or catchphrases in prospect-facing text, and never implies a tie to Sandler, Black Swan Group, Gartner or anyone else.

**Facts every example line obeys.** Prices, terms, guarantees and live offers come only from the offer section of projects/<project>/CONTEXT.md, with their real dates. Discounts only if they are live there. Money outcomes say "could". Proof only from the allowed-proof section of CONTEXT.md. The examples below use the fictional "Brightpane Window Co." (first clean $149, then from $99 a visit, cancel anytime) or {{placeholders}}.

## 0. Which framework when (pick one lead, borrow from the others)

| Moment | Lead framework | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Opening a call or a reply thread | Sandler up-front contract | Lowers guard, gives them a clean "no" |
| They named a problem and stopped | Sandler pain funnel, SPIN implication | Takes it from "a thing" to "what it costs me" |
| You need the full picture before an offer | Gap Selling | Where they are, where they want to be, the distance in their numbers |
| They're tense, skeptical, or got burned | Voss labels, accusation audit | Name the fear before it blocks the talk |
| They think they have no problem ("we're fine") | Challenger teach | One insight about their own business they hadn't seen |
| Writing a follow-up or a proof line | Cialdini (honestly) | Reciprocity and consistency, real proof only |
| Deciding what to ask next on a call | SPIN | Few fact questions, many problem and consequence questions, value question last |

Rule of thumb: one framework move per message. Two in one sentence reads like a script.

## 1. Gap Selling (Keenan)

**The idea.** People don't buy a product. They buy the move from how things are to how they want them to be. Before offering anything, map three things:
- **Where they are now.** Plain facts first (how work comes in, who handles it, what slips, what a job is worth), then how they feel about it.
- **Where they want to be.** In their words, not your feature list.
- **The distance.** How big the difference is, ideally in numbers they gave you. No distance, no sale, and that's fine.

A useful discovery habit from the book: for each problem, note what it costs them and what causes it. A problem with no cost isn't worth solving. The cause tells you whether your offer even touches it.

**Discovery questions (generic, adapt per project):**
1. "Walk me through what happens when a new customer reaches out and you're busy."
2. "What happens to that request after that?"
3. "How often is that, in a normal week? In your busiest week?"
4. "What's a typical job worth to you?"
5. "Why do you think those people don't follow up?" (cause)
6. "If that were sorted, what would your week look like?" (where they want to be)
7. "What does leaving it as it is cost you over a year?" (the distance)

**When to use.** Any discovery call, and any reply where they gave one fact and you need two more before an offer makes sense.

**Example lines (Brightpane, selling to a property manager):**
- "So right now the windows get done when someone complains, and tenants notice first. Is that about right?"
- "If the glass was just clean every month without you thinking about it, what would change for you?"
- "You said two tenant complaints a month and each one eats an hour of your day. That's what we'd be working on."

**Misuse to avoid.** Inflating the current state to make the distance look bigger. Using numbers they never gave. Building a gap they don't feel. If it's small, say so and walk away.

## 2. Sandler (pain funnel, up-front contract, negative reverse)

**The idea.** Pushing creates pushback. Agree on how the conversation will go first, let the buyer find and size their own problem, and when they lean away, don't chase.

**2a. Up-front contract.** At the start, agree out loud on how long, what each side wants from it, and how it ends (yes, no, or a dated next step). Make it explicit that "no" is an acceptable answer.

- "I've got 15 minutes. I'll ask a few questions, then tell you honestly if I think we can help. That work?"
- "If it's not for you, just say so at the end. If it fits, we'll pick a start date. OK?"
- "Anything you want to be sure we cover first?"

**2b. Pain funnel.** A short run of questions that moves from the surface of a problem, to an example, to what it costs, to how they feel about it. One question at a time, and only go deeper while they keep giving. Our condensed version:
1. Ask them to say more.
2. Ask for a recent, specific example.
3. Ask what they've already tried and how it went.
4. Ask what it costs them, roughly.
5. Ask how they feel about it now.

Use it only after the owner names a problem on their own. Stop at the first step where answers go short. With a busy owner, three steps is plenty.

- "You said Mondays are the worst. Say more?"
- "What happened the last time it went wrong?"
- "How do you feel about it when that happens?"

**2c. Negative reverse.** When they pull back, step back with them instead of pushing. Said calmly and meant honestly, it lets them argue for the fix themselves.

- "Sounds like you've got this covered and it may not be for you. Fair?"
- "If your current setup keeps you full, you may not need us at all."
- "Honestly, at that volume I'd tell you to keep your money."

**Misuse to avoid.** Negative reverse as a trick (if they agree, let them go). Digging for emotional pain to make someone feel bad. Using the up-front contract to corner them later. The contract protects their right to say no, not your right to demand an answer.

## 3. Chris Voss (labels, mirrors, calibrated questions, accusation audit)

**The idea.** People decide once they feel understood. Show you understand before you argue anything. Name what they seem to feel or want, out loud, without agreeing or judging.

- **Label.** Start with "It sounds like..." or "It seems like...", name the feeling or situation, then stop talking. Skip "I hear you" and "I understand".
- **Mirror.** Repeat their last few key words as a soft question. It keeps them talking without you steering.
- **Calibrated question.** Open "how" or "what" questions that make them think through the problem with you. Avoid "why" (sounds like blame) and yes/no questions.
- **Accusation audit.** Say the worst things they might be thinking about you before they say them. It takes the sting out.
- **Aim for agreement with your summary.** A good summary gets "that's right" (they feel heard). "You're right" usually means they want you to stop.

**When to use.** Objections, skeptical replies, anyone burned by a past vendor, tense moments, and the gap summary.

**Example lines:**
- Label: "Sounds like you've paid for this kind of thing before and couldn't tell if it worked."
- Mirror: Owner: "We tried a service like this. Total headache." Martha: "A headache?"
- Calibrated: "What would need to be true for this to be worth {{price}} to you?"
- Accusation audit: "You might be expecting a long contract and a big setup fee. Fair worry. Here's how we actually do it: {{terms from CONTEXT.md}}."
- Summary: "So the work is there, you're stretched thin, and the requests that come in while you're busy are the ones that get away. Did I get that right?"

**Misuse to avoid.** Labeling feelings to fake closeness. Mirroring to steer (BELFORT.md already bans covert mirroring of body language). An accusation audit listing fears you can't actually answer. Using calibrated questions to squeeze on price. If your price is fixed per CONTEXT.md, don't pretend it's negotiable.

## 4. Challenger (teach, tailor, take control)

**The idea.** CEB's research (Dixon and Adamson, roughly 6,000 reps) found that top performers teach buyers something about their own business, fit it to their situation, and calmly hold their ground on value. A good insight is true, is about their business, changes how they see the problem, and leads naturally back to what you offer.

A simple flow: start in their world, reframe what's really happening, show it in their own numbers, make it personal, then offer the fix.

**Insights must be true and sourced.** Keep project-specific insights in projects/<project>/CONTEXT.md. A general example with a public source:
- Speed often matters more than price on a first contact. A large lead-response study found firms that responded to web leads within an hour were far likelier to reach a decision maker than those that waited longer (Harvard Business Review, 2011; see SOURCES.md). Better said on a call than in cold email.

**When to use.** "We're fine" owners, word-of-mouth owners, and the opening of a call where there's no felt problem yet. Not with someone already in pain: they need listening, not teaching.

**Example lines (Brightpane, to a storefront owner):**
- Teach: "Most shop owners think about windows as cleaning. Shoppers read dirty glass as 'maybe closed'."
- Tailor: "You said most of your sales are walk-ins. That's exactly where the glass matters."
- Take control: "I'd rather not drop the price. If it doesn't pay for itself, it's not a fit, and I'll tell you that."

**Misuse to avoid.** Teaching with stats you can't source. Making the owner feel dumb. "Take control" turning into pressure or arguing past a no. Insights that are really about you. Never invent an industry generalization.

## 5. SPIN (Rackham)

**The idea.** Huthwaite's research (roughly 35,000 sales calls) found that in larger sales, successful sellers ask four kinds of questions in a rough order, and ask few of the first kind.
- **Situation.** Facts. Ask as few as possible, and never one you could look up yourself.
- **Problem.** What's difficult or unsatisfying.
- **Implication.** What that problem leads to. This is where small problems grow. Strong sellers ask the most of these.
- **Need-payoff.** Gets the buyer to say the value of fixing it, in their words. Much stronger than you saying it.

**When to use.** Planning a discovery call's question list, and choosing the one question in a reply.

**Example lines:**
- Problem: "When you're busy, how do new requests get handled?"
- Implication: "When a request sits for a day, where does that customer go?"
- Need-payoff: "If every request got a reply within the hour, what would that be worth to you in a month?"

Situation, only if you truly can't find it: "Is it just you and one crew right now?"

**Misuse to avoid.** Piling on implication questions until it feels like an interrogation. Leading questions dressed as need-payoff ("Wouldn't you love more jobs?"); RUBRIC.md scores those low on curiosity. Asking situation questions you could have answered from your research wastes their time.

## 6. Cialdini's principles (ethical use only)

Seven principles from Influence (the 2021 revised edition added unity). Each can be used honestly or as a trick. Use only the honest version, with true facts.

| Principle | Honest use | Example line | Never |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reciprocity | Give something useful first, like a real observation about their business | "I walked past your shop Tuesday. Wanted to mention the front glass, either way." | "Gifts" with strings, a "free audit" that's really a pitch |
| Commitment and consistency | Small, real yeses that match what they already said | "You said Mondays are the worst. Want to start there?" | Trapping them with their own words, yes-ladders |
| Social proof | Only proof from the allowed-proof section of CONTEXT.md, labeled, where your rules allow it | On a call: "Here's one result, shared with the client's OK." | Invented clients, reviews, counts |
| Authority | Show you know their world through specifics | "Spring pollen season is when your glass looks worst." | Credentials, badges or partnerships you don't have |
| Liking | Genuine curiosity, their name, real common ground | "Your reviews keep mentioning how fast you answer. That's rare." | Flattery, fake shared interests |
| Scarcity | Only a dated offer from CONTEXT.md that really expires | "The first-clean price runs until {{real date}}." | "Only 2 spots left", rolling deadlines, made-up demand |
| Unity | Shared identity only when true (both small, owner-run, local) | "We're a small shop too. We answer our own phone." | Pretending to belong to their community or culture |

**When to use.** Follow-ups and closing turns. One principle per message, never stacked.

**Misuse to avoid.** Everything in the "Never" column. If a line only works because the prospect doesn't know something, cut it.

## 7. Quick checks before using any framework line

1. Is the fact in the prospect record, the thread, or CONTEXT.md? If not, cut it.
2. Is it one move, one question, one ask?
3. Would it still feel fair if the prospect read this file? If not, it's manipulation. Cut it.
4. In cold email, does it break your project's copy rules? Then it belongs on the call instead.
