## 1. The value equation (Hormozi's)

Value goes up with the dream outcome and how likely they think it is to work. It goes down with how long it takes and how much effort it costs them.

| Lever | Raise it honestly by |
|---|---|
| Dream outcome | Naming the result in their words, not the tool |
| Likelihood | Their own evidence (something you checked on their business), real guarantees, a clear price, allowed proof only |
| Time to first win | Shortening the path: "clean windows this week" beats "onboarding" |
| Effort | Doing it for them; they keep their tools and habits |

The bottom half (time, effort) is usually cheaper to improve, and it needs no claim you can't back.

Example (fictional Brightpane Window Co.): "We bring everything, you don't need to be home, and the first clean is $149."

## 2. Building the offer

- **Price on value, not on being cheapest.** Make the value obviously bigger than the number so the comparison is "this vs. doing nothing", not "this vs. the cheapest rival". Martha never changes a price; she frames the one in CONTEXT.md.
- **Build it:** pick the outcome, list every obstacle before, during and after, turn each into a solution, choose how it's delivered, then cut anything costly and low-value.
- **Scarcity and urgency only when true:** a dated offer that really expires, real capacity limits. Never fake counts or timers (the FTC treats fake countdowns as deceptive).
- **Bonuses over discounts.** Each one removes one specific obstacle. List only what is really delivered.
- **Guarantees.** Common types: unconditional refund, conditional (refund or extra work if they did their part), "all sales final" stated as a strength, and pay-when-it-works. Two different kinds together are stronger than one. Martha states only the guarantees written in CONTEXT.md, word for word, never bigger.
- **Naming.** A good offer name gives a reason, names who it's for, a goal, a time frame and a container word (kit, plan, system). Internal only until the owner approves a public name.

## 3. Getting leads

- **Four channels:** warm outreach, content, cold outreach, paid ads. Start with the cheapest and closest; add one only when the current one works.
- **Warm outreach:** people the owner already knows, asked who has the problem. Martha drafts; the owner sends.
- **Cold outreach:** show you looked at *them*, give something useful up front, use a list that fits, protect deliverability, follow up more than once. Each follow-up adds something new, never "just bumping". Cadence and send caps come from PLAYBOOK.md and CONTEXT.md, not from here.
- **Lead magnets:** a free thing that solves a small real problem and points to the bigger one: a diagnosis or audit, a sample or trial, or one step of the process. Easy to consume, obvious next step.
- **Volume and patience:** most outreach fails from too little activity for too short a time. Use a daily activity target as a goal, never as a reason to break caps, suppression lists or quality gates.
- **More, better, new:** do more of what works, then fix the weakest stage, then add a channel. Test one change at a time; record results in LESSONS.md.

## 4. Short sales call (CLOSER, adapted to about 15 minutes)

His point (via summaries): people don't buy because they blame circumstances (time, money), other people (partner, crew) or themselves (avoiding the decision, a past failure). Expect more than one layer. Most selling happens in discovery.

| Step | Roughly | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Clarify | 0-3 min | Why are they here, why now |
| Label | 3-4 | Name the problem more precisely than they did; get a yes |
| Overview past pain | 4-7 | What they tried, what it cost, why it didn't fix it |
| Sell the after-picture | 7-9 | The outcome in their words, not features |
| Explain away concerns | 9-13 | Find the real concern, answer it, ask again once |
| Reinforce | 13-15 | Confirm the decision, next steps, who does what, when |

State price and terms exactly as in CONTEXT.md, ask for the decision plainly, accept "no", and send a short recap within a day.

## 5. Money models (general)

Offers usually play one of four roles: getting a stranger to buy (attraction), adding a related purchase (upsell), a smaller option instead of a discount (downsell), and ongoing service (continuity). The idea he stresses: early cash from a new customer should cover what it cost to get them. The owner decides all of these; Martha only describes what is live in CONTEXT.md.

## 6. Objections (honest version)

Pattern: **acknowledge** what they said, note that it's a sensible thing to ask (never invent "a customer just like you"), **ask** one question that finds the real concern, answer it with a fact, then ask for the decision once more.

- **"Too expensive."** Find out if it's cash or doubt about value. Value: compare to what the problem costs them. Cash: restate the real terms and any live offer; never a price that isn't in CONTEXT.md.
- **"No time."** Show how little they have to do. One consequence question at most: "When it's this busy, what slips?"
- **"Need to ask my partner."** Ask early who else decides. Offer a short call with both.
- **"Need to think."** "Fair. Is it the price, the timing, or whether it'll work for you?" Answer that one thing. Mention a deadline only if it is real and live.
- **"Tried this before."** Ask what broke, then show the difference using their answer, not hype.
- **"Send me info."** Ask what they'd need to see, send exactly that, plus one dated next step.

## What Martha uses, adapts, bans

- **Uses:** the value equation, offer building, honest guarantees and bonuses, the channel list, lead magnets, the call structure, the objection pattern.
- **Adapts:** volume targets become goals under existing caps; urgency and scarcity only when real and dated; guarantees only as written in CONTEXT.md.
- **Bans:** invented clients, results, counts, reviews or deadlines; pushing after a clear no; arguing with a stated budget; promising income or results ("could", never "will"); his name, persona or catchphrases in anything a prospect sees.
