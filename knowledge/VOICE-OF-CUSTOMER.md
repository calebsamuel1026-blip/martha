# Voice of customer: how to find and use your buyers' own words

Martha is your sales agent across projects. Copy works better when it sounds like the buyer talking, not like the seller. This file is the method for gathering voice of customer (VOC) for any project, plus worked generic examples from home services and small business. Store each project's findings in projects/<project>/CONTEXT.md (voice section), not here.

## 1. Where to find buyer words

Work down this list until you have 20-30 real phrases. Note the source type next to each phrase.

1. **Your own conversations (best).** Replies to your emails, call notes, objections, questions people asked before buying, cancellation reasons. These beat any public source because they are your buyers.
2. **Reviews of the thing they buy now or used to buy.** Trustpilot, Google, G2, Capterra, BBB, app stores. One- and two-star reviews show pains and distrust; four- and five-star reviews show what earns trust. Read competitors' reviews too.
3. **Trade forums and communities.** Industry forums, Reddit communities for the trade or role, Facebook groups, Q&A threads. Look for "has anyone tried...", "got burned by...", "how do you handle...".
4. **Podcasts and video comments** in the trade. Episode titles and listener questions show what owners worry about this season.
5. **Vendor and trade articles.** Useful for the list of complaints they summarize, but they are written to sell. Treat their phrasing as "how buyers are described as talking", not buyer speech, and treat their numbers as unverified unless they cite a study.
6. **Job posts and the buyer's own website.** What they brag about (fast response, family-owned, licensed and insured) tells you what they think their customers value.

**Access caveat.** Some forums block automated reading or sit behind logins and paywalls. Say so in your notes. If the phrases came only from vendor articles, mark the whole set as low confidence and have someone with a logged-in browser skim the real communities before copy goes out at scale.

## 2. How to capture it

- **Paraphrase by default.** Keep a short quoted phrase only when the exact wording matters (under 15 words, with the source type).
- **No personal data.** Never store a poster's name, handle, business name, phone or address. You want the words, not the person.
- **Tag each phrase:** source type (own reply, review, forum, vendor article), date seen, and whether it is verbatim or described.
- **Count repeats.** A pain that shows up across three source types is real. One vivid quote is an anecdote.
- **Keep the numbers honest.** A statistic goes into copy only with a public source you can name (see RESEARCH-EVIDENCE.md). Vendor figures without a study stay out.

## 3. What to extract (the template)

Fill these six sections per project.

1. **Top pains, in their terms.** What costs them money, time or sleep. Use their nouns ("the phone rings while I'm on a ladder"), not yours ("lead leakage").
2. **Objections and what is underneath.** A two-column table: what they say, and the fear or need behind it.
3. **Trust and distrust.** What makes them walk away from a seller, and what makes them lean in.
4. **Words to use, avoid, and handle carefully.**
5. **Buying triggers.** The events that make them look for help this week rather than someday.
6. **Message rules.** Five to ten rules that turn the above into copy decisions.

## 4. Worked example A: home-service owners buying marketing or lead services

A generic example built from public reviews of lead vendors, trade forum posts and trade articles about roofers, plumbers, HVAC and similar contractors. Paraphrased. Verify against your own replies before relying on it.

### Pains
1. **Leads that are not real.** Wrong numbers, people who never asked for a quote, jobs outside the service area. The plain version: "I paid for garbage."
2. **Shared leads.** Several contractors show up for the same job; the cheapest or fastest wins and the rest paid for nothing.
3. **Missing calls while on a job.** Customers rarely leave voicemail; they call the next name on the list.
4. **Paying twice for the same failure.** Ad spend creates calls, then the calls are lost to bad routing, a full voicemail or no after-hours coverage.
5. **Locked out of their own stuff.** An agency holds the domain, the Google Business Profile or the tracking number.
6. **Price shoppers** who only want the lowest bid.
7. **Can't get out.** Long terms, cancellation fees, auto-renewals, refund fights.
8. **Nobody says what happened.** Reports full of impressions and rankings, not calls and signed jobs.
9. **Being pestered.** Vendors who keep calling after being told to stop.
10. **Seasonal price spikes** when everyone in the area wants the same work.

### Objections and what is underneath

| What they say | What is underneath |
|---|---|
| "I've been burned by a marketing company before." | Paid, saw nothing, felt foolish. Needs proof and an easy exit more than another promise. |
| "I don't have time for this." | Often true, and often a polite no. A five-minute, no-setup path beats "a quick call". |
| "We get plenty of work from referrals." | Pride and fear of changing what works. Don't argue; show what sits on top of referrals. |
| "How much? What's the contract?" | Was locked in before. Wants a clear price and a short or month-to-month term. |
| "Is this AI? Customers hate bots." | Reputation is everything. Wants it to sound like the company and hand real decisions to a person. |
| "Someone in the office answers the phone." | Control and trust. Ask what happens after hours or when that person is busy. |
| "Send me info." | Usually a brush-off. Treat it as a no unless they name a next step. |
| "Is this one of those lead companies?" | Distrust of shared leads. Answer in the first line. |
| "What happens when I cancel?" | Wants to keep everything: numbers, site, profiles, data. |
| "Will it hold up in the busy season?" | Capacity fear: more work than they can handle anyway. |

### Trust and distrust
**Distrust:** long terms with no performance target; guaranteed rankings or "first page" promises; a slick rep who promises everything; templated pages and reports nobody can explain; "exclusive" leads that aren't; the vendor owning the customer's assets; jargon with no names and no references to call; refunds the customer has to chase.

**Trust:** short or month-to-month terms; the customer owns their domain, profiles, numbers and data; paying for something countable (calls answered, appointments booked, jobs signed); specific local proof they can verify; a named person with a callback number; short plain messages that sound like a tradesperson; fast response; tools that work from a phone in the field.

### Words
**Use:** missed calls, on a job, quote request, estimate, booked, signed job, call back, your phone number, your Google profile, month to month, you own it, no long contract, plus the trade's own nouns (for a roofer: inspection, leak, storm damage, insurance claim; for HVAC: no-heat call, tune-up, service agreement).

**Avoid:** funnel, pipeline velocity, omnichannel, synergy, leverage, "AI-powered solution", lead nurture, ROI optimization, impressions, brand awareness, digital transformation, growth partner, "scale your business", "we're not like other agencies", "just following up", "quick call".

**Handle carefully:** "AI" (say what it does, not what it is). "Leads" (many hear "shared junk"; say "people who asked you for a quote"). "Guarantee" (only if specific and in writing).

### Buying triggers
A weather event or seasonal rush; a slow month (open to looking, price-sensitive); the office person quits; a lost job they can name; a vendor contract that just ended badly; ad spend that isn't turning into jobs; a bad review that cost work; planning before the spring or fall rush; a growth step (second crew, new area) where the owner can no longer answer every call.

### Message rules for this audience
1. Open with a specific job or money loss in their world, not a company intro.
2. Say early what you are not (not shared leads, not a long contract), if that is true for your offer.
3. State terms plainly. Never bury a long commitment.
4. Write like a tradesperson to a tradesperson: short sentences, no buzzwords, no exclamation marks.
5. Count jobs and calls, not impressions or rankings.
6. Say what they keep and how they leave.
7. When "AI" appears, pair it with the human handoff and say what it will not do.
8. Use seasonal or after-hours timing only when it is true for that market. Never invent urgency.
9. One low-effort ask (a one-word reply). Don't ask them to learn software.
10. Respect a no immediately and once. Being the seller who actually stops is a differentiator. Follow your project's copy rules (projects/<project>/CONTEXT.md, optional COPY-RULES.md) for format.

## 5. Worked example B: homeowners buying a recurring home service

Fictional, for illustration: Brightpane Window Co., residential and commercial window cleaning in Maple Falls. Offer: $149 first clean, then from $99 per visit, cancel anytime. These phrases are hypotheses to check against real reviews of local window cleaners and the business's own customer replies.

- **Pains in their words:** "streaks after the last guy", "they never showed", "I can't get to the second-floor windows", "screens got bent", "they tracked dirt through the house".
- **Objections:** "How much for my house?" (wants a number before a visit), "Are you insured?" (fear of damage or injury on their property), "Do I have to sign up for a plan?" (fear of being locked in).
- **Trust:** a clear starting price, insured and named crew, photos of real local jobs, a re-clean promise if streaks show up, cancel anytime.
- **Distrust:** a price that only appears after a visit, pushy plan upsells, no-shows, vague arrival windows.
- **Words to use:** streak-free, inside and out, screens and tracks, on time, same crew. **Avoid:** "premium solutions", "elevate your home", "exclusive membership".
- **Triggers:** spring cleaning, before a party or a house sale, after construction, pollen season.
- **Rule this produces:** lead with the first-clean price and the cancel-anytime term, since price uncertainty and lock-in are the two loudest objections. This is an example of risk reversal, not a claim about any real business.

## 6. Example C: software or tools sold to small operators

When the product is a tool (a scheduling app, a booking tool, an invoicing app), buyers in any trade tend to share the same worries:

- **Speed and time saved** is the hook, stated in their units (jobs booked, hours back, invoices paid), not features.
- **Account or platform risk:** "Will this get my account flagged?" If the tool touches a third-party platform, never promise it is safe or "ban-proof". Say plainly what it does, that credentials and the account stay theirs, and that they must follow the platform's terms.
- **Use their words, not yours:** pick the nouns they use daily in the trade; avoid words that sound like misuse ("scraping", "botting", "hack", "exploit").
- **Low-risk start:** a trial and cancel-anytime beat any feature list.

## 7. Honest limits

- Public VOC is often secondhand. Mark what is verbatim and what is described.
- Vendor articles carry conversion figures that are rarely sourced. Do not quote them as fact.
- Your own replies outrank everything here. Update the project's voice section as real conversations come in, and log which phrases earned replies.
